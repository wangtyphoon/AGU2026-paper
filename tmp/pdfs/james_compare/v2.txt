Abstract 19 
Artificial intelligence weather prediction (AIWP) has achieved strong performance in pointwise 20 
accuracy and tropical cyclone track forecasting, yet many models substantially underestimate 21 
cyclone intensity and exhibit deficiencies in physical consistency. These limitations constrain 22 
their use in research-oriented atmospheric simulations. We develop a regional AIWP framework 23 
for the western North Pacific that combines a multiscale mesh, a broad set of atmospheric and 24 
surface variables, and a message-passing graph neural network with linear attention. 25 
Predictability-dependent filtering and spectral constraints are introduced during training to 26 
mitigate excessive smoothing and preserve mesoscale variability. Relative to a model trained 27 
solely with pointwise mean squared error, the spectrum-aware variants improve tropical cyclone 28 
intensity forecasts as verified against ERA5 while largely preserving track accuracy, at the cost 29 
of a modest increase in pointwise error. Structural diagnostics also show improved 30 
representations of inner-core dynamics and thermodynamic structure. In 30-day autoregressive 31 
integrations with ERA5-derived lateral boundary forcing, the models retain organized cyclone 32 
circulations and remain stable in most cases despite not being explicitly trained for these longer 33 
horizons. Stronger spectral retention is accompanied by a modest reduction in long-term 34 
stability. These results demonstrate the value of spectrum-aware training for improving tropical 35 
cyclone forecasts and support further investigation of its use in extended atmospheric simulations 36 
and other extreme weather applications. 37

Plain Language Summary 45 
Artificial intelligence can produce accurate weather forecasts, but its predictions often 46 
underestimate tropical cyclone strength and blur mesoscale atmospheric features. These 47 
shortcomings limit its use in studying how storms evolve. We develop a regional weather model 48 
for the western North Pacific that uses a broad set of atmospheric and surface variables. We also 49 
adjust how the model learns from its errors so that it balances forecast accuracy with the 50 
preservation of atmospheric features across different spatial scales. This approach improves 51 
forecasts of cyclone intensity relative to the reference reanalysis while largely maintaining track 52 
accuracy. It also produces storm circulations and temperature structures that more closely 53 
resemble those in the reference data. In monthly simulations supplied with boundary conditions 54 
from reanalysis, the models can retain organized storms, although preserving stronger mesoscale 55 
variability can reduce stability. These results suggest a way to improve AI-based cyclone 56 
forecasts and support longer atmospheric simulations, while highlighting the need to evaluate 57 
performance under realistic forecast boundary conditions. 58

1 Introduction 69 
Tropical cyclones (TCs) are among the most destructive weather systems, causing substantial 70 
economic damage and loss of life worldwide. Predicting TC track, intensity, and structure 71 
remains a major forecasting challenge. Reliable forecasts are essential for anticipating TC-related 72 
hazards and supporting risk assessment and preparedness (Powell & Reinhold, 2007). Alongside 73 
advances in high-resolution, physics-based numerical weather prediction (NWP), data-driven 74 
models have become increasingly important in weather forecasting. These approaches span 75 
applications ranging from statistical post-processing and downscaling to global autoregressive 76 
models capable of producing medium-range forecasts. 77 
 78 
Data-driven weather forecasting has advanced through several distinct modeling paradigms. 79 
Early graph neural network–based systems demonstrated forecast skill approaching that of state-80 
of-the-art NWP models, despite operating at relatively coarse spatial resolution (Keisler, 2022). 81 
Subsequent studies demonstrated competitive medium-range forecast skill using markedly 82 
different architectures, including adaptive Fourier neural operators (Pathak et al., 2022), 83 
hierarchical three-dimensional Transformer architectures (Bi et al., 2023), and multiscale graph 84 
representations. Progress has also depended on training methods and data strategies. GraphCast 85 
combined multiscale representations with multi-step rollout training (Lam et al., 2023), whereas 86 
Aurora leveraged heterogeneous data and large-scale pretraining to extend data-driven prediction 87 
across multiple Earth-system applications (Bodnar et al., 2025). Together, these developments 88 
show that diverse architectures can achieve high forecast skill and underscore the importance of 89 
training strategy, objective formulation, and data composition (Garg et al., 2026). Consequently, 90 
further advances in AI weather prediction may depend not only on model architecture but also on 91 
the properties of atmospheric variability that training objectives encourage models to retain. 92 
 93 
Although data-driven weather prediction models have substantially improved conventional 94 
verification metrics, including root-mean-square error (RMSE) and anomaly correlation 95 
coefficient (ACC), these metrics provide only a partial characterization of forecast quality 96 
(Zhang et al., 2026). Such aggregate accuracy measures may obscure important forecast 97 
deficiencies, including systematic underestimation of TC intensity, biases in storm structure and 98 
cross-scale variability, and increasingly oversmoothed forecast fields at longer lead times (L. A. 99

Smith & Thorpe, 2026). These deficiencies can substantially limit the practical value of data-100 
driven models for TC forecasting. Despite competitive track forecasts, data-driven models still 101 
lag behind operational guidance in TC intensity forecasting (DeMaria et al., 2025; Liu et al., 102 
2024). This gap is particularly pronounced for extreme events that require generalization beyond 103 
commonly observed conditions, including super typhoons and TC rapid intensification (Chih et 104 
al., 2026; Sun et al., 2025). 105 
 106 
The training objective may contribute to these deficiencies, particularly excessive smoothing and 107 
underestimated intensity. Most deterministic data-driven weather prediction models are trained 108 
primarily to minimize pointwise mean squared error (MSE). However, the conditional mean that 109 
minimizes expected MSE does not necessarily correspond to a physically realizable atmospheric 110 
state. As forecast uncertainty increases, the conditional distribution broadens, and its mean may 111 
increasingly average over multiple plausible atmospheric evolutions rather than represent an 112 
individual realization (Selz et al., 2026). For spatially varying weather systems, such averaging 113 
can attenuate sharp gradients and small-scale structures, thereby producing progressively 114 
smoother forecast fields. A spectral perspective further clarifies this limitation. In a spherical 115 
harmonic representation (assuming orthonormalized basis functions), MSE can be decomposed 116 
into contributions associated with differences in spectral amplitude and phase (Bouallègue, 117 
2024).For a fixed spherical harmonic degree 𝑙, the contribution to the squared error can be 118

 128 
Spatial displacement of a forecast feature appears spectrally as a phase change. Even when the 129 
amplitude of a displaced feature is accurately reproduced, pointwise verification penalizes the 130

forecast both where the observed feature is absent from the forecast and where the displaced 131 
forecast feature appears. This is the double-penalty effect of pointwise verification. As a 132 
feature’s location becomes more uncertain, reducing its amplitude can lower the expected 133 
pointwise squared error by reducing the penalty for misplaced high-amplitude structures. 134 
Pointwise MSE optimization can therefore favor reduced forecast activity as predictability 135 
decreases, a behavior commonly described as the accuracy–activity trade-off (Bouallègue, 2024).  136 
 137 
Consistent with this mechanism, several AI weather prediction systems exhibit reduced spectral 138 
variability relative to reanalysis, particularly at smaller spatial scales and longer forecast lead 139 
times. This tendency can become especially pronounced in models trained with multi-step 140 
autoregressive rollouts (Philippé et al., 2025), even though autoregressive curriculum training is 141 
commonly employed to improve forecast accuracy and long-term stability (Bodnar et al., 2025; 142 
Lam et al., 2023). For TC prediction, improved model design may improve predictability and 143 
mitigate excessive smoothing; however, substantial biases remain in the representation of inner-144 
core structure and dynamics (Weng & Gori, 2026). These include deficiencies in TC intensity, 145 
wind–pressure relationships, radial wind structure, radius of maximum wind, and dynamical 146 
balance. Together, these results suggest that improvements in conventional forecast accuracy do 147 
not necessarily imply improvements in TC structure and dynamics. 148 
 149 
These limitations motivate training objectives that explicitly account for spectral properties. 150 
NeuralGCM, for example, incorporates lead-time-dependent spectral filtering together with 151 
spectral amplitude and bias constraints within its deterministic training formulation (Kochkov et 152 
al., 2024). Other approaches include the binned spectral power (BSP) loss, which constrains the 153 
distribution of power across spatial scales (Chakraborty et al., 2026), and the adjusted mean 154 
squared error (AMSE), which separates spectral amplitude errors from decorrelation errors to 155 
reduce the incentive for excessive smoothing (Subich et al., 2025). These approaches provide a 156 
foundation, but preserving spectral power alone does not ensure accurate feature placement, 157 
realistic TC structure, or consistent relationships among variables. Moreover, spectral 158 
formulations developed for global domains, particularly those based on spherical harmonics, 159 
must be adapted to limited-area regional grids and boundary-forced prediction systems. 160

A central question is whether preserving spectral variability in a fully data-driven regional model 162 
improves TC intensity and structure, and at what cost to pointwise forecast accuracy. Addressing 163 
this question requires more than improved power spectra: spectral fidelity must be evaluated 164 
together with pointwise forecast error and TC-specific structural diagnostics. 165 
 166 
We investigate this accuracy–variability–structure trade-off using a fully data-driven regional 167 
deep-learning framework for the western North Pacific. We develop a regional graph neural 168 
network using ERA5 reanalysis and a controlled training framework for comparing spectral 169 
objectives with conventional pointwise optimization (Hersbach et al., 2020). The model 170 
combines multiscale graph representations with a broad set of atmospheric and surface variables, 171 
and the training procedure progressively extends forecast lead time and introduces predictability-172 
dependent spectral filtering and constraints on spectral variability. We evaluate whether these 173 
objectives improve TC representation using three complementary classes of diagnostics: 174 
conventional pointwise verification, spectral measures of atmospheric variability, and TC-175 
specific measures of track, intensity, and storm structure. This design tests whether greater 176 
spectral activity is accompanied by physically meaningful improvements in simulated storms. 177 
 178 
Section 2 describes the data and methodology. Section 3 outlines the evaluation framework. 179 
Section 4 evaluates model performance using synoptic-scale metrics. Section 5 presents tropical 180 
cyclone verification, including track, intensity, and storm structure. Section 6 presents case 181 
studies and extended-range simulations. Section 7 discusses the main findings, limitations, and 182 
implications. 183

(Hersbach et al., 2020). We use a regional rather than global domain to limit computational cost 188 
while retaining sufficient interior area for evaluating predictive skill. The temporal subset 189 
includes periods extending three days before and after times when a TC’s best-track position lies 190

To better represent atmospheric interactions, we include a broader set of variables than the 202 
simplified dynamical fields used in previous studies. This choice reflects the coupled physical 203 
processes governing TC evolution, including oceanic energy supply (Emanuel, 1986), boundary-204 
layer angular momentum transport (R. K. Smith & Montgomery, 2025), eyewall replacement 205 
cycles, and terrain-induced potential vorticity changes (Wang, 2012; C.-C. Wu & Liao, 2026). 206 
These interactions are also relevant to TC genesis, during which cloud–radiative feedbacks (Hsu 207 
& Wu, 2025), mid-level vorticity (Loi et al., 2024), and other environmental conditions may 208 
jointly determine whether an incipient disturbance organizes into a mature TC (Tseng et al., 209 
2026). Including variables associated with these processes provides a broader physical basis for 210 
learning coupled TC evolution. The variables used in this study are summarized in Table 2. 211 
All fields are sampled at six-hour intervals. For all accumulated variables, the values represent 212 
accumulations over the preceding one-hour interval. Missing sea surface temperature and 213 
significant wave height values are replaced by 270 K and 0 m, respectively. During data 214 
preprocessing, spatial nearest-neighbor filling is applied to sparse missing surface values, and 215 
bilinear interpolation is used to map wave height onto the atmospheric grid. Temporal inputs 216 
include the local-solar-time ratio, the annual-phase ratio, and the sine and cosine transforms of 217 
both quantities. Predicted SST and wave height are fed back as inputs at subsequent 218 
autoregressive steps within the domain interior, whereas lateral boundary values are prescribed 219 
from ERA5.  220

2.2 Graph Representation and Model Architecture 223 
An appropriate spatial representation is essential for modeling atmospheric evolution. Numerical 224 
weather prediction models use configurations including nested domains in WRF and variable-225 
resolution meshes in MPAS-A to allocate computational resources to regions that require finer 226 
resolution (Park et al., 2014). In this study, the ERA5 fields are provided on a regular 0.25° 227 
latitude–longitude grid. Although the angular grid spacing is uniform, the physical spacing varies 228 
with latitude because meridians converge toward the poles (Figure 1a and 1b). We therefore 229 
distinguish between the grid on which atmospheric inputs and predictions are defined and the 230 
graph representation on which latent features are processed. 231 
 232 
Existing AI weather prediction models employ different spatial representations and 233 
computational architectures. Pangu-Weather and Aurora use three-dimensional architectures 234 
derived from the Swin Transformer (Bi et al., 2023; Bodnar et al., 2025), whereas the spherical 235 
Fourier neural operator approach developed for FourCastNet processes atmospheric fields in the 236 
spectral domain (Bonev et al., 2023). Here, we adopt an icosahedral graph representation similar 237 
to that used in GraphCast (Lam et al., 2023). Unlike GraphCast, which combines connections 238 
from multiple refinement levels into a multimesh graph, we retain each refinement level as a 239 

=== PAGE 11 ===
manuscript submitted to Journal of Advances in Modeling Earth Systems 
 
separate graph. This design processes latent atmospheric features at distinct spatial resolutions 240 
and exchanges them through explicit interlevel connections. 241 
 242 
Graph neural networks process information through message passing between connected nodes 243 
(Battaglia et al., 2018). In our formulation, edge features are updated from the states of 244 
connected nodes, and the resulting messages are aggregated to update node states. Encoding 245 
relative geometry in the edge features allows these updates to account for both atmospheric 246 
conditions and the spatial relationships between interacting locations. This mechanism supports 247 
learning of spatially coupled dynamics, consistent with previous applications of graph neural 248 
networks to physical simulation (Pfaff et al., 2020; Sanchez-Gonzalez et al., 2020). 249 
 250 
The graph hierarchy comprises five icosahedral refinement levels (3–7), containing 38, 156, 618, 251 
2,441, and 9,749 nodes, respectively (Figure 1e–i). Together with the latitude–longitude grid, 252 
these meshes form a heterogeneous graph with distinct node types and connectivity patterns. To 253 
reduce truncation of graph neighborhoods near the lateral boundaries, mesh nodes are retained 254 
over an extended domain with a 2.5° buffer around the atmospheric grid (Figure 1c and 1d). The 255 
buffer supplies additional nodes for connections near the domain edges without enlarging the 256 
atmospheric prediction domain. 257 
 258 
Separate connectivity rules govern encoding, processing, decoding, and information exchange 259 
between mesh levels. During encoding, grid nodes are connected to level-7 mesh nodes within a 260 
prescribed great-circle distance to transfer atmospheric information from the regular grid to the 261 
mesh (Figure 1j). Within each mesh level, processor edges connect adjacent mesh nodes and 262 
include self-loops; Figure 1k illustrates a level-7 node connected to its six neighbors and itself. 263 
During decoding, each grid node receives information from its three nearest mesh nodes at the 264 
relevant refinement level to project learned mesh features back onto the latitude–longitude grid 265 
(Figure 1l). 266 
 267 
Successive mesh levels exchange information through pooling and upsampling connections in a 268 
graph U-Net structure (Gao & Ji, 2022). These connections link coarse-level nodes to seven-269 
node neighborhoods on the finer mesh. Pooling aggregates fine-level information onto the 270

Each graph neural network layer exchanges information within the first-order neighborhood 313 
defined by the graph. Stacking multiple GATv2 layers progressively expands the receptive field 314 
(Brody et al., 2021), allowing information to propagate over larger spatial distances (Sanchez-315 
Lengeling et al., 2021). Mesh levels 3–7 use 6, 8, 12, 16, and 16 local GATv2 layers, 316 
respectively. At each level, a parallel branch uses one linear-attention layer to capture broader 317 
spatial interactions (Q. Wu et al., 2023). Both branches use 512-dimensional latent features and 318 
single-head attention. Their outputs are combined through a learnable sigmoid gate, followed by 319 
layer normalization. Each level has a separate gate parameter initialized to 0.5, giving an initial 320 
local-branch weight of approximately 0.622. 321 
 322 
We incorporate several components to improve graph-based learning and training stability, 323 
including edge features, normalization, dropout, residual connections, feed-forward networks, 324 
and positional encodings (Luo et al., 2024, 2025). Layer normalization supports stable training 325 
with relatively small batch sizes (Ba et al., 2016). A gated linear unit replaces the conventional 326 
feed-forward network to provide feature-dependent gating and nonlinear feature transformations. 327

2.3 Training 342 
Training proceeds in four stages to balance forecast accuracy and physical consistency. Stage 1 343 
pretrains the model on a coarser mesh, and Stage 2 fine-tunes it on a finer mesh. Stage 3 extends 344 
training from single-step predictions to multistep autoregressive rollouts. Stage 4 adds 345 
predictability-dependent filtering and spectral losses to the objective. This progression moves 346 
from learning the large-scale environment to resolving finer-scale features, maintaining stability 347 
over longer integrations, and preserving the distribution of spectral variability. 348 
 349 
The training hyperparameters are summarized in Table 3. Prediction targets are formulated as 350 
atmospheric-state increments. Loss weights account for latitude-dependent grid-cell area and the 351 
relative importance and predictability of different variables. 352 
 353 
The treatment of lateral boundary conditions (LBCs) is important for regional autoregressive 354 
models because boundary errors can propagate into the interior through advection or wave 355 
propagation (Warner et al., 1997). In numerical models using semi-Lagrangian advection, for 356 
example, the lateral boundary buffer must span several grid points to support numerical stability 357 
(Davies, 2014). In our configuration, ERA5 lateral boundary forcing is applied over a nine-grid-358 
point buffer beginning at the second autoregressive step. 359

The predictability-filtered MSE objective is designed to retain accuracy at predictable scales 408

We compare the MSE-only baseline with two spectrum-aware variants that penalize absolute or 414

3 Evaluation 420 
All evaluations use the 2024 test set, following the tropical-cyclone-centered sampling criterion 421 
described in Section 2.1. Forecasts are initialized at 00, 06, 12, and 18 UTC. We evaluate three 422 
variants of the proposed model: the Stage 3 checkpoint trained solely with the weighted MSE 423 
objective and two Stage 4 variants incorporating the Spectrum-MAE and Spectrum-MSE 424 
formulations, respectively. 425 
 426 
For external comparison, Pangu-Weather serves as a deterministic reference (Bi et al., 2023). 427 
Although more recent deterministic AI weather prediction systems, such as GraphCast and 428 
Aurora (Bodnar et al., 2025; Lam et al., 2023), have demonstrated improved performance in 429 
conventional global forecast metrics, including RMSE, Pangu-Weather is useful for the 430

4 Synoptic-Scale Metrics 516 
We use specific humidity and meridional wind as representative synoptic-scale variables because 517 
they characterize TC moisture distributions and wind fields. To assess these aspects of TC 518 
structure and environment, we supplement RMSE and ACC with forecast bias and activity. 519 
These complementary metrics help distinguish retained atmospheric variability from 520 
improvements that may primarily reflect calibration. Here, intrinsic skill refers to the model’s 521 
ability to reproduce the observed atmospheric evolution and variability, whereas calibration 522 
denotes improvements that can be achieved through procedures such as smoothing, filtering, or 523 
systematic bias correction (Bonavita & Geer, 2026). 524 
 525 
As shown in Figure 3b, the regional model trained with the pointwise MSE objective exhibits 526 
approximately 15% lower RMSE for the 850-hPa meridional wind than Pangu-Weather at the 527 
first forecast step, before any ERA5 lateral boundary updates are applied during the rollout. This 528 
advantage generally narrows initially and widens at longer lead times, when ERA5 boundary 529 
forcing may contribute to the regional model’s performance, precluding an equal-information 530 
comparison with Pangu-Weather. Relative to the pointwise MSE control, Spectrum-MAE and 531 
Spectrum-MSE generally exhibit higher RMSE, with smaller differences in ACC. For 700-hPa 532 
specific humidity, both Pangu-Weather and the MSE control show increasingly negative biases. 533 
at longer lead times, indicating growing moisture underestimation relative to ERA5.  534

 804 
The comparison between the two spectral-loss formulations does not identify a consistently 805 
superior formulation, particularly because their normalization schemes differ. Nevertheless, the 806 
results reveal distinct characteristics. Under their respective normalization schemes, the 807 
Spectrum-MAE configuration better preserves medium-range forecast accuracy and high-808 
wavenumber features in short- and medium-range forecasts, whereas the Spectrum-MSE 809 
configuration more effectively retains the overall spectral-energy distribution during month-long 810 
autoregressive integrations, at the cost of greater degradation in conventional accuracy metrics 811 
and reduced long-term stability. This contrast further illustrates the trade-off between pointwise 812 
forecast accuracy and the preservation of dynamically relevant variability and consistency 813 
(Bouallègue, 2024). Future work could combine these objectives or transition between them 814 
across forecast time scales. More broadly, these results underscore the importance of training 815 
objectives and data alongside model architecture (Garg et al., 2026). 816 
 817 
Several limitations qualify these findings. The regional simulations remain constrained by 818 
reanalysis-derived lateral boundary forcing. Consequently, the experimental framework is more 819 
comparable to a regional WRF-style simulation than to a fully operational forecasting system, 820 
limiting direct comparisons of absolute forecast accuracy with global operational models. In 821 
addition, the primary references for TC intensity and structural diagnostics are derived from 822 
reanalysis. The reanalysis does not fully resolve the most intense and compact TC inner cores, 823 
introducing uncertainty into the evaluation. The diagnosed improvements therefore primarily 824 
describe agreement with ERA5 within the present regional, boundary-forced configuration. 825 
 826 
Despite the benefits of spectral training, the framework still depends on manually specified 827 
choices. Future work should assess and reduce sensitivity to the filter cutoff wavenumber, the 828
