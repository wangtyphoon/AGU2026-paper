Abstract
Artificial intelligence weather prediction (AIWP) has demonstrated strong performance in
minimizing pointwise mean squared error (MSE) and predicting tropical cyclone tracks. Despite
these advances, maintaining physical consistency remains a challenge, and existing AIWP
models often substantially underestimate tropical cyclone intensity. These limitations constrain
their applicability to research-oriented atmospheric simulations. This study develops an AIWP
framework that uses a multiscale mesh to represent atmospheric processes across spatial scales
and incorporates a comprehensive set of atmospheric state variables. The model combines a
message-passing graph neural network with linear attention to capture spatial interactions and
long-range dependencies. During training, predictability-dependent filtering and spectral
constraints are introduced to mitigate excessive smoothing and preserve high-frequency features.
The resulting framework improves tropical cyclone intensity forecasts without compromising
track accuracy. Further analysis shows that models trained with spectral constraints better
preserve physical consistency in tropical cyclone inner-core dynamics and thermodynamic
structure. Even without explicit training for longer forecast horizons, the model maintains stable
forecasts and physically realistic structures during extended autoregressive integration. These
results highlight the value of predictability-dependent filtering and spectral constraints for
improving tropical cyclone prediction and suggest their potential applicability to other extreme
weather events and long-term simulation experiments.

Plain Language Summary
Artificial intelligence can produce accurate global weather forecasts. However, it often
underestimates tropical cyclone intensity and blurs fine-scale atmospheric features. These
limitations restrict the broader application of AI weather models to studying tropical cyclone
evolution. In this study, we develop an AI weather prediction model that incorporates a
comprehensive set of atmospheric variables. We also design an adjusted loss function to balance
forecast accuracy and atmospheric activity. This approach significantly improves tropical
cyclone intensity forecasts while maintaining track accuracy. It also better preserves physical
consistency, producing stable predictions with realistic cyclone structures. These findings offer a
general framework for improving AI-based tropical cyclone forecasts, with promising
applications to other extreme weather events and extended atmospheric simulations.

1 Introduction
Tropical cyclones (TCs) are among the most destructive weather systems, causing substantial
socioeconomic losses and loss of life worldwide. Accurately predicting TC evolution, including
changes in track, intensity, and structure, remains a major forecasting challenge. Reliable
forecasts of these characteristics are therefore essential for anticipating TC-related hazards and
supporting risk assessment and preparedness (Powell & Reinhold, 2007). In parallel with
continued advances in high-resolution physics-based numerical weather prediction (NWP), data-74
driven deep-learning approaches have emerged as an increasingly important component of
modern weather forecasting. These approaches span a broad range of applications, from
statistical post-processing and downscaling to global autoregressive models capable of producing
medium-range atmospheric forecasts.

The development of data-driven weather forecasting has proceeded through several distinct
modeling paradigms. Early graph neural network–based systems demonstrated forecast skill
approaching that of state-of-the-art NWP models, despite operating at relatively coarse spatial
resolution (Keisler, 2022). Subsequent studies showed that competitive medium-range forecast
skill could be achieved using markedly different architectures, including adaptive Fourier neural
operators (Pathak et al., 2022), hierarchical three-dimensional Transformer architectures (Bi et
al., 2023), and multiscale graph representations. Importantly, progress has also resulted from
advances in training methodology and data strategy. GraphCast combined multiscale
representations with multi-step rollout training (Lam et al., 2023), whereas Aurora leveraged
heterogeneous data and large-scale pretraining to extend data-driven prediction across multiple
Earth-system applications (Bodnar et al., 2025). Collectively, these developments demonstrate
that high forecast skill can emerge from diverse architectural paradigms and increasingly
highlight the importance of training strategy, objective formulation, and data composition
alongside architectural innovation (Garg et al., 2026). Consequently, further advances in AI
weather prediction may depend not only on how forecasting models are constructed, but also on
which properties of atmospheric variability their training objectives encourage them to retain.

Although data-driven weather prediction models have achieved substantial improvements in
conventional verification metrics, such as root-mean-square error (RMSE) and anomaly

=== PAGE 5 ===


correlation coefficient (ACC), these metrics provide only a partial characterization of forecast
quality (Zhang et al., 2026).  Such aggregate accuracy measures may obscure important forecast
deficiencies, including systematic underestimation of tropical cyclone intensity, biases in storm
structure and cross-scale variability, and increasingly over-smoothed forecast fields at longer
lead times (L. A. Smith & Thorpe, 2026). These deficiencies can substantially limit the practical
value of data-driven models for tropical cyclone forecasting. Despite their highly competitive
track-prediction performance, data-driven models still lag substantially behind operational
guidance in tropical cyclone intensity forecasting  (DeMaria et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024). This
gap in intensity forecasting capability is particularly pronounced in extreme cases that require
stronger generalization and extrapolation beyond commonly observed conditions, such as super
typhoons and tropical cyclone rapid intensification events (Chih et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2025).

The choice of training objective may contribute to some of these deficiencies, particularly the
tendency toward excessive smoothing and weakened forecast intensity. Most deterministic data-112
driven weather prediction models are trained predominantly using pointwise mean squared error
(MSE), with minimization of this objective commonly treated as a direct route toward improving
forecast accuracy. However, the conditional mean that minimizes expected MSE does not
necessarily correspond to a physically realizable atmospheric state.  As forecast uncertainty
increases, the conditional distribution broadens, and its mean may increasingly represent an
average over multiple plausible atmospheric evolutions rather than any individual realization
(Selz et al., 2026). For spatially varying weather systems, such averaging can attenuate sharp
gradients and small-scale structures, thereby producing progressively smoother forecast fields.
This limitation can also be understood from a spectral and spatial-error perspective. In a
spherical harmonic representation, MSE can be decomposed into contributions associated with
differences in spectral amplitude and phase (Bouallègue, 2024).

Spatial displacement of a forecast feature is expressed spectrally through phase changes. Even
when the amplitude of a displaced feature is accurately reproduced, pointwise verification
penalizes the forecast both where the observed feature is absent from the forecast and where the
displaced forecast feature appears. This constitutes the familiar double-penalty effect of
pointwise verification. When the location of a feature becomes increasingly uncertain, reducing
its amplitude can lower the expected pointwise squared error by reducing the penalty associated
with misplaced high-amplitude structures. Pointwise MSE optimization can therefore favor
reduced forecast activity as predictability decreases, a behavior commonly described as the
accuracy–activity trade-off (Bouallègue, 2024).

Consistent with this mechanism, several evaluated AI weather prediction systems exhibit
deficient spectral variability relative to reanalysis, particularly at smaller spatial scales and
longer forecast lead times. This tendency can become especially pronounced in models trained
with multi-step autoregressive rollouts (Philippé et al., 2026), even though autoregressive
curriculum training is commonly employed to improve forecast accuracy and long-term stability
(Bodnar et al., 2025; Lam et al., 2023). For tropical cyclone prediction, improved model design
may partially enhance predictability and mitigate the adverse effects of excessive smoothing;
however, substantial biases remain in the representation of inner-core structure and dynamics
(Weng & Gori, 2026). These include deficiencies in TC intensity, wind–pressure relationships,
radial wind structure, radius of maximum wind, and dynamical balance. Together, these results
suggest that improvements in conventional forecast accuracy do not necessarily imply
corresponding improvements in the structural and dynamical characteristics of tropical cyclones.

These limitations have motivated training objectives that explicitly account for spectral
properties. NeuralGCM, for example, incorporates lead-time-dependent spectral filtering
together with spectral amplitude and bias constraints within its deterministic training formulation
(Kochkov et al., 2024).  Other approaches include the binned spectral power (BSP) loss, which
constrains the distribution of power across spatial scales (Chakraborty et al., 2026), and the

adjusted mean squared error (AMSE), which separates spectral amplitude errors from
decorrelation errors to reduce the incentive for excessive smoothing (Subich et al., 2025). These
approaches provide important foundations, but preserving spectral power alone does not ensure
accurate feature placement or improved tropical cyclone structure and cross-variable
relationships. Moreover, spectral formulations developed for global domains, particularly those
based on spherical harmonics, require modification when applied to limited-area regional grids
and boundary-forced prediction systems.

A central unresolved question is therefore whether improved preservation of spectral variability
in a fully data-driven regional forecast model translates into meaningful improvements in
tropical cyclone intensity and structure, and what trade-off, if any, this imposes on pointwise
forecast accuracy. Addressing this question requires more than demonstrating improved power
spectra alone: spectral fidelity must be evaluated together with pointwise forecast error and TC-172
specific structural diagnostics.

Our study investigates this accuracy–variability–structure trade-off using a fully data-driven
regional deep-learning framework for the western North Pacific. We develop a regional graph
neural network using ERA5 reanalysis and construct a controlled training framework in which
spectral objectives can be evaluated alongside conventional pointwise optimization (Hersbach et
al., 2020). The model includes multiscale graph representations and a broad set of atmospheric
and surface variables, while the training procedure progressively extends forecast lead time and
introduces predictability-dependent spectral filtering and constraints on spectral variability. We
evaluate whether these objectives improve the representation of tropical cyclones using three
complementary classes of diagnostics: conventional pointwise verification, spectral measures of
atmospheric variability, and TC-specific measures of track, intensity, and storm structure. This
design allows us to determine whether increased spectral activity is accompanied by physically
meaningful improvements in the simulated storms rather than by spectral power increases alone.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the data and
methodology. Section 3 outlines the evaluation framework. Section 4 evaluates model
performance using synoptic-scale metrics. Section 5 presents tropical cyclone verification,

2 Data and Methodology
2.1 Dataset
The dataset is constructed from ERA5 reanalysis data on a 0.25° latitude–longitude grid
(Hersbach et al., 2020).  To balance computational cost while preserving a sufficiently effective
area for evaluating predictive skill, we use a regional domain rather than a global domain. We
select a temporal subset comprising periods extending three days before and after times when a
tropical cyclone’s best-track position lies within the selected domain. This selection reduces the
number of samples by approximately half while retaining the atmospheric evolution during and
around the tropical cyclone lifetime. The data are divided into separate training, validation, and
test sets, with all evaluations reported in this study conducted on the 2024 test set. Three-203
dimensional atmospheric variables are represented across 13 pressure levels. Climatological
reference fields used for selected evaluation metrics are obtained from WeatherBench (Rasp et
al., 2024), while tropical cyclone data are obtained from the Joint Typhoon Warning Center
(JTWC) best-track dataset. Further details of the dataset are summarized in Table 1.

As stated earlier, to better represent the full range of atmospheric components and their
interactions, we incorporate a broader set of atmospheric variables, extending beyond the
simplified dynamical fields considered in previous studies. This choice is motivated by
established understanding of tropical cyclone evolution, which depends on a range of coupled
physical processes, including oceanic energy supply (Emanuel, 1986), boundary-layer angular
momentum transport (R. K. Smith & Montgomery, 2025), eyewall replacement cycles and
terrain-induced potential vorticity changes (Wang, 2012; C.-C. Wu & Liao, 2026). Representing
these interactions is also relevant to tropical cyclone genesis, during which cloud–radiative
feedbacks (Hsu & Wu, 2025), mid-level vorticity (Loi et al., 2024), and other environmental
conditions may jointly determine whether a tropical cyclone seed becomes sufficiently organized
to develop into a mature tropical cyclone (Tseng et al., 2026). Including variables associated
with these processes provides a broader physical basis for learning the coupled evolution of
tropical cyclones. The variables used in this study are summarized in Table 2.

2.2 Graph and Model
An appropriate spatial representation is essential for modeling atmospheric evolution. Numerical
weather prediction models use configurations such as nested domains in WRF and variable-228
resolution meshes in MPAS-A to allocate computational resources to regions requiring finer
resolution (Park et al., 2014). In this study, the ERA5 fields are provided on a regular 0.25°
latitude–longitude grid. Although this grid offers a consistent representation of the atmospheric
variables, its physical spacing varies with latitude because meridians converge toward the poles
(Figure 1a and 1b). We therefore distinguish between the grid on which atmospheric inputs and
predictions are defined and the graph representation on which latent features are processed.

Existing AI weather prediction models employ different spatial representations and
computational architectures. Pangu-Weather and Aurora use three-dimensional architectures
derived from the Swin Transformer (Bi et al., 2023; Bodnar et al., 2025), whereas the spherical
Fourier neural operator approach developed for FourCastNet processes atmospheric fields in the
spectral domain  (Bonev et al., 2023). Here, we adopt an icosahedral graph representation similar
to that used in GraphCast (Lam et al., 2023). Unlike GraphCast, which combines connections
from multiple refinement levels into a multimesh graph, we retain each refinement level as a
separate graph. This design allows latent atmospheric features to be processed at distinct spatial
resolutions and exchanged between levels through explicit interlevel connections.

Graph neural networks process information through message passing between connected nodes
(Battaglia et al., 2018). In the formulation used here, edge features are updated using the states of

The graph hierarchy comprises five icosahedral refinement levels, from levels 3 to 7, containing
38, 156, 618, 2,441, and 9,749 nodes, respectively (Figure 1e and 1i). Together with the latitude–256
longitude grid, these meshes form a heterogeneous graph with distinct node types and
connectivity patterns. To reduce the truncation of graph neighborhoods near the lateral
boundaries, mesh nodes are retained over an extended domain with a 2.5° buffer around the
atmospheric grid (Figure 1c and 1d). This buffer provides additional mesh nodes for connections
near the domain edges without enlarging the atmospheric prediction domain.

Connectivity is defined separately for encoding, processing, decoding, and information exchange
between mesh levels. During encoding, grid nodes are connected to level-7 mesh nodes within a
prescribed great-circle distance, transferring atmospheric information from the regular grid to the
mesh (Figure 1j). Within each mesh level, processor edges connect adjacent mesh nodes and
include self-loops; Figure 1k illustrates a level-7 node connected to its six neighbors and itself.
During decoding, each grid node receives information from its three nearest mesh nodes at the
relevant refinement level, projecting the learned mesh features back onto the latitude–longitude
grid (Figure 1l).

Information exchange between successive mesh levels is implemented through pooling and
upsampling connections in a graph U-Net structure (Gao & Ji, 2022). These connections
associate coarse-level nodes with local neighborhoods on the finer mesh, using seven-node
neighborhoods to transfer features between resolutions. Pooling aggregates fine-level
information onto the coarser mesh, whereas upsampling transfers coarse-level information back
to the finer mesh. Together with message passing within each level, these operations allow the
model to combine information represented at multiple spatial resolutions.

Building on this multi-level graph representation, the overall model uses a hierarchical Graph U-298
Net architecture shown in Figure 2 (Gao & Ji, 2022). The architecture consists of five major
stages: encoding, pooling, processing, upsampling, and decoding. Atmospheric information is
first encoded from the latitude–longitude grid onto the graph, progressively transferred across
different mesh resolutions, processed within the multiscale graph hierarchy, and then propagated
back to the grid through the upsampling and decoding stages. Because atmospheric evolution is
governed by interactions of varying strengths among surrounding atmospheric states, this study
adopts GATv2 (Brody et al., 2021) as the primary graph neural network layer. The module’s
dynamic attention mechanism lets the model adaptively adjust the relative importance of
surrounding atmospheric information based on the evolving atmospheric state.

In a graph neural network, each layer exchanges information within the first-order neighborhood
defined by the graph connectivity. Stacking multiple GATv2 layers progressively expands the
receptive field (Brody et al., 2021), allowing information to propagate over larger spatial
distances (Sanchez-Lengeling et al., 2021). During the processing stage, a dual-branch design is
introduced to balance computational cost and long-range information exchange. One branch
performs graph-based message passing, while the other employs linear attention to capture
broader spatial interactions (Q. Wu et al., 2023). The outputs of the two branches are then
combined through a gated fusion mechanism.

Several architectural components are incorporated to improve the effectiveness and stability of
graph-based learning, including edge features, normalization, dropout, residual connections,
feed-forward networks, and positional encodings (Luo et al., 2024, 2025). Layer normalization is
adopted to maintain stable training under relatively small batch sizes (Ba et al., 2016). The feed-321
forward network is replaced by a gated linear unit to introduce feature-dependent gating and
enhance nonlinear feature transformation.

2.3 Training
To achieve an optimal trade-off between physical consistency and accuracy, the model training
procedure is divided into four stages. In the first stage, pre-training is conducted on a coarser-351
resolution mesh. The second stage fine-tunes the model on a finer-resolution mesh. In the third
stage, the training extends from single-step predictions to autoregressive multi-step rollouts.
Finally, the fourth stage incorporates predictability-based filtering and spectral loss functions
into the optimization objective. The model is trained progressively according to task difficulty
advancing from large-scale background environments to mesoscale details, then to long-term
simulation stability, and ultimately to a physically consistent atmospheric energy distribution.

The training hyperparameters are summarized in Table 3. The prediction targets are consistently
formulated as increments of the atmospheric state. In addition to area-weight adjustments that
account for latitude-dependent grid distortions, loss weighting incorporates the relative
importance and predictability of different variables.

For a regional autoregressive model, lateral boundary condition (LBC) treatment is critical for
maintaining simulation stability, as errors at the boundaries can propagate into the interior
domain via advection or wave propagation (Warner et al., 1997). Taking semi-Lagrangian
advection as an example, the lateral boundary buffer must span at least several grid points to
ensure numerical stability (Davies, 2014). In our training configuration, ERA5 lateral boundary
forcing is applied over a nine-grid-point buffer beginning at the second autoregressive step.

In contrast to NeuralGCM (Kochkov et al., 2024), which uses forecast skill from a physical
model to determine the filtering threshold and applies a fixed 12th-order filter, this study derives
the filtering criterion directly from the spectral characteristics of the Stage 3 model trained solely
with the weighted MSE objective. Specifically, the energy distribution over the training set is
used as the reference, and the cutoff wavenumber is defined as the scale beyond which the model
spectrum exhibits more than a 10% reduction in energy relative to ERA5. This choice is
motivated by the close relationship between the spectral energy decay induced by MSE
optimization and the reduction in predictability at progressively smaller spatial scales.

In the subsequent analysis, we compare formulations optimized using solely MSE against those
incorporating spectral losses under two distinct norm formulations. These two norms correspond

=== PAGE 18 ===


to absolute and squared error losses, with the principal difference lying in the gradients they
impose on spectral errors: the squared formulation assigns substantially higher gradient weights
to larger errors, thus dominating the optimization direction. Although the Earth is spherical, the
Fourier transform is employed here as a planar approximation alongside radial wavenumber
binning under the assumption of statistical isotropy.

Ultimately, the predictability-filtered MSE objective is designed to preserve predictability while
mitigating excessive damping of the forecasted energy spectrum. As a result, the spectral error
loss directly constrains the atmospheric energy distributions, while the spectral bias penalty
curbs systematic spectral drift, thereby enhancing the physical consistency of the rollouts.
3 Evaluation
All evaluation results presented in this study are computed over the 2024 test set, following the
tropical-cyclone-centered sampling criterion described in Section 2.1. Forecasts are initialized at
00, 06, 12, and 18 UTC. Three variants of the proposed model are evaluated: the Stage 3
checkpoint trained solely with the weighted MSE objective, and two Stage 4 variants
incorporating the Spectrum-MAE and Spectrum-MSE formulations, respectively.

For external comparison, Pangu-Weather is selected as the deterministic baseline (Bi et al.,
2023). Although more recent deterministic AI weather prediction systems, such as GraphCast
and Aurora (Bodnar et al., 2025; Lam et al., 2023), have demonstrated improved performance in
conventional global forecast metrics, including RMSE, Pangu-Weather remains particularly
suitable for the objectives of this study. It offers lower computational overhead, and previous
studies have extensively examined its tropical cyclone forecasts, providing a solid reference for
interpreting results (DeMaria et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024; Weng & Gori, 2026). Furthermore,
Pangu-Weather does not employ the multi-step autoregressive curriculum training commonly
adopted in later systems and is therefore less affected by the progressive accumulation of
excessive smoothing over extended autoregressive rollouts (Philippé et al., 2026). Its
comparatively moderate spectral dissipation makes it a suitable baseline for evaluating whether
the proposed spectrum-aware objectives can effectively retain mesoscale atmospheric energy and

=== PAGE 19 ===


improve the physical consistency of the forecast fields, rather than merely reducing conventional
pointwise forecast errors.

Although the nine-grid-point boundary buffer is excluded from our analysis, prescribed ERA5
boundary forcing can still influence the interior domain during autoregressive integration.
Accuracy metrics from the second forecast step onward therefore characterize a boundary-442
constrained regional system and should not be interpreted as a comparison with Pangu-Weather
under identical information constraints. Accordingly, Pangu-Weather serves as an external
reference, while the effects of spectrum-aware training are assessed primarily through
comparisons among the three variants of the proposed model under the same lateral boundary
conditions.

quantified using two conventional measures, the root-mean-square error (RMSE) and anomaly
correlation coefficient (ACC), which respectively evaluate pointwise forecast errors and the
spatial correspondence of atmospheric anomalies. Forecast bias and forecast activity serve as
indirect diagnostics of physical consistency by assessing systematic deviations from the
reference state and the magnitude of spatial variability, respectively (Bonavita & Geer, 2026).
Together, these metrics provide a complementary framework for determining whether the
spectrum-aware objectives improve the representation of atmospheric variability without
substantially degrading conventional forecast accuracy. The corresponding formulations are

4 Synoptic-Scale Metrics
Among the synoptic-scale atmospheric variables, specific humidity and meridional wind are
selected as representative variables because they directly characterize the moisture distribution
and wind field associated with tropical cyclones. To evaluate the model’s practical skill in
reproducing these aspects of tropical-cyclone structure and environment, the conventional RMSE
and ACC metrics are supplemented with forecast bias and forecast activity. These
complementary metrics are used to distinguish intrinsic forecast skill from improvements that
may arise primarily through calibration. Here, intrinsic skill refers to the model’s ability to
reproduce the observed atmospheric evolution and variability, whereas calibration denotes
improvements that can be achieved through procedures such as smoothing, filtering, or
systematic bias correction (Bonavita & Geer, 2026).

As shown in Figure 3, after the prescribed lateral boundary conditions begin to influence the
forecasts, our model exhibits RMSE values approximately 10% lower than those of Pangu-530
Weather, indicating comparable or higher pointwise accuracy under the present evaluation
framework. This advantage initially decreases with forecast lead time and subsequently increases
again, possibly because the influence of the lateral boundary conditions gradually propagates
into the interior of the regional domain. Comparing the different training objectives, both
Spectrum-MAE and Spectrum-MSE generally exhibit higher RMSE than the MSE-trained
model, whereas their effects on ACC are relatively moderate. For specific humidity, both Pangu-536
Weather and the MSE-trained model show pronounced dry biases at longer lead times,
suggesting that their moisture distributions become progressively less consistent with the
reanalysis field and limiting the fidelity of their moisture forecasts at longer lead times.

While forecast activity confirms that optimizing solely for pointwise accuracy metrics reduces
atmospheric variability, this metric has inherent limitations. It cannot resolve the specific spatial
scales at which this loss of variability occurs, let alone quantify its impact on the sharpness of
mesoscale features. To address this, Figure 5 presents a spectral analysis based on the ratio of
modeled energy spectra to that of the reanalysis field across wavenumbers, with total energy
summed over wavenumbers corresponding to wavelengths shorter than 1000 km to assess
cumulative energy changes at mesoscale ranges.

On the first forecast day, the energy decay is relatively moderate and confined to smaller spatial
scales; as lead time increases, this energy loss progressively intensifies and expands toward
larger spatial scales. This behavior aligns well with theoretical expectations, as the degree of
energy decay is closely tied to atmospheric predictability. Notably, this degradation is far more
pronounced in total mesoscale energy. The models trained exclusively with pointwise accuracy
metrics—including both the external baseline and our internal pointwise variants—exhibit severe
energy decay, retaining only approximately 60–75% of the reanalysis energy by day 7,
depending on the variable. Visually, this loss of energy manifests as progressively blurred
meteorological features, which is particularly detrimental to mesoscale severe weather systems
such as tropical cyclones (Ebert-Uphoff et al., 2025).

In sharp contrast, the model variants constrained by the spectral loss function demonstrate a
significantly milder energy decline, with this advantage widening as lead time increases.
Furthermore, their mesoscale energy remains largely stable throughout the temporal evolution.
These results demonstrate that incorporating a spectral constraint positively contributes to
preserving the overall energy distribution—especially at smaller spatial scales—thereby ensuring
the consistency and comparability of forecast fields across varying lead times.

5 Tropical Cyclone Verification
To quantitatively assess the impact of spectral-loss training on tropical cyclone (TC) forecasts,
we apply the TC tracking algorithm described in Section 3 and evaluate forecasts using a
common-sample framework. Because a regional forecasting system may introduce sampling
biases when TCs enter or leave the model domain, or when storms develop or dissipate during
the forecast period, only cases successfully identified across all four model configurations are
retained. The evaluation is conducted at forecast lead times of 1–3 days, comprising 265, 211,
and 162 forecast samples, respectively, corresponding to 18, 16, and 15 individual TCs.

The results demonstrate that incorporating spectral loss during training improves TC intensity
prediction while largely preserving track accuracy (Figure 7). TC motion is primarily governed
by large-scale environmental steering flows; consequently, the inclusion of spectral constraints
produces only minor differences in absolute track error. In contrast, TC intensity is more
sensitive to the representation of mesoscale structures. Consistent with the enhanced spectral
energy retention at these scales discussed in the preceding sections, the spectral-loss models
exhibit improved intensity forecasts relative to both the MSE-only baseline and Pangu-Weather,
with substantial reductions in intensity error.

Beyond the mean error statistics, spectral-loss training also improves the representation of the
distribution of TC intensity changes. In particular, the spectral-loss models better reproduce the
upper tail of the ERA5 intensity-change distribution, whereas pointwise-loss forecasts tend to
underrepresent strongly intensifying cases. Preserving this tail behavior is especially relevant for
forecasts of rapid intensification, for which excessive smoothing of mesoscale structures may
systematically suppress large intensity changes. These results therefore provide TC-scale
evidence that the improved retention of mesoscale spectral energy translates into more accurate
representations of storm intensity and intensification.

We further evaluate the representation of TC structure using a set of structural parameters,
focusing on both their overall distributions and their behavior in the distribution tails (Figure 9).
We quantify structural accuracy using root-mean-square error (RMSE), while we assess
differences in the full distributions using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) test (Massey Jr., 1951).
Because the forecast and reanalysis samples are paired and include repeated forecasts of
individual TCs, the reported standard KS p-values do not account for these dependencies and
should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of distributional agreement. Tail behavior is
summarized using the 5th percentile for MSL, Rmax, and B, and the 95th percentile for the
remaining parameters. These lower-tail diagnostics represent low central pressure, compact inner
cores, and the negative tail of the signed asymmetry parameter, respectively. As shown in
Section 4, spectral energy decays at different rates for specific humidity and wind, indicating
variable-dependent smoothing behavior. Similar differences are evident among the TC structural
diagnostics.

For the wind–pressure relationship, models trained solely with pointwise accuracy loss exhibit a
systematic weak-intensity bias. The underestimate is more pronounced for axisymmetric
maximum wind speed than for minimum sea level pressure, resulting in distortions of the wind–680
pressure relationship. This discrepancy is also reflected in the gradient-wind-balance diagnostics.
In the reanalysis, intense TCs exhibit departures from gradient-wind balance, with the wind-682
inferred geopotential-height difference exceeding the directly diagnosed difference for some
cases. Supergradient tangential winds would produce an overestimate of the geopotential-height
difference when substituted into the gradient-wind-balance relation. The spectral-loss models
reproduce the joint Vmax–MSL distribution and the relationship between the directly diagnosed
and wind-inferred geopotential-height differences more closely than the MSE-only model.
Consistently, the KS tests yield larger p-values for the spectral-loss models, indicating that the
tests do not detect statistically significant differences from the reanalysis distributions for several
of these structural variables. Their tail quantiles are also generally closer to those of the
reanalysis, demonstrating improved representation of the strongest storms and the most
pronounced departures from gradient-wind balance.

To demonstrate differences persist beyond conventional medium-range forecast horizons, we
conduct month-long autoregressive simulations for 96 initial conditions. Each simulation is
integrated for 30 days while retaining ERA5-derived lateral boundary forcing. Figure 13 and
Figure 14 summarize the evolution of spectral energy during these simulations for 850-hPa wind
and 700-hPa specific humidity. Across the 30-day integrations, the spectrum-MSE model
consistently retains a larger fraction of the reanalysis spectral energy than the spectrum-MAE
model. The separation between the two models generally increases with forecast lead time.
The enhanced spectral-energy retention of the spectrum-MSE model is accompanied by a modest
reduction in long-term numerical stability. Among the total cases, four spectrum-MSE
simulations become unstable, compared with one unstable case for the spectrum-MAE model.
The results indicate a trade-off between retaining higher-frequency variability and limiting the
growth of forecast perturbations over extended integration periods.
For a qualitative examination of the month-long simulations, we select the forecast initialized at
late October. This period is characterized by active tropical cyclone genesis over the western
North Pacific, providing a demanding test of whether the models can maintain realistic structures
over several weeks. As shown in Figure 14, both spectrum-aware models continue to simulate
organized tropical cyclone circulations even at forecast lead times approaching 27 days. The
simulations contain repeated episodes of cyclone development, intensification, and dissipation
rather than a progressive collapse toward an excessively smooth background state.
Differences between the two spectral-loss formulations are evident in the spatial fields.
Consistent with the spectral diagnostics, the spectrum-MSE model maintains stronger mesoscale
variability in both the moisture and wind fields. Tropical cyclone circulations are generally more
intense, and the surrounding specific-humidity fields exhibit sharper spatial gradients than in the
spectrum-MAE simulation. One possible explanation is the different error weighting inherent to
the two spectral objectives. For a squared-error formulation, the magnitude of the loss gradient
increases with the spectral error, assigning stronger corrections to spectral components with large
amplitude discrepancies, whereas the absolute-error formulation does not exhibit this error-790
dependent gradient amplification.

=== PAGE 40 ===


In the monthly simulations, the spectrum-aware models are able to reproduce the evolution of
multiple TCs under reanalysis-derived lateral boundary forcing. This behavior differs from that
commonly observed in deterministic pointwise-trained models, whose practical value at
extended lead times can be substantially reduced as progressive smoothing suppresses mesoscale
variability.

The comparison between the two spectral-loss formulations does not indicate an unambiguous
overall winner, particularly because their normalization schemes are not completely identical.
Nevertheless, the results reveal distinct characteristics. The spectrum-MAE formulation is better
suited to preserving medium-range forecast accuracy and high-wavenumber sharpness, whereas
the spectrum-MSE formulation retains the overall spectral-energy distribution more effectively
during long autoregressive integrations, at the cost of larger degradation in conventional
accuracy metrics. This contrast further illustrates the trade-off between pointwise forecast
accuracy and the preservation of dynamically relevant variability and consistency (Bouallègue,
2024). A possible direction for future work is therefore to combine or transition between these
objectives across different forecast time scales. Broadly, the results suggest that training
objectives and data impact forecast behavior just as strongly as model architecture (Garg et al.,
2026).

Several limitations should nevertheless be considered when interpreting these results. The
forecasts examined here remain constrained by reanalysis-derived lateral boundary forcing.
Consequently, the experimental framework is more comparable to a regional WRF-style
simulation than to a fully operational forecasting system, limiting direct comparisons of absolute
forecast accuracy with global operational models. In addition, the primary references for TC
intensity and structural diagnostics are derived from reanalysis. Because the effective resolution
of the reanalysis is insufficient to fully resolve the most intense and compact TC inner-core
structures, these reference fields also introduce uncertainty into the evaluation. Despite these
limitations, the results provide useful guidance for the further development of regional AIWP
systems and spectrum-aware training strategies.


=== PAGE 41 ===


Although the spectral-loss configurations considered in this study produce substantial differences
relative to the original pointwise-accuracy objective, several aspects of the training framework
remain dependent on manually specified choices. Future work should therefore aim to reduce
this dependence, including the selection of the filtering cutoff wavenumber, the relative
coefficients of individual loss components during spectrum-aware training, and the weighting
assigned to different forecast variables. It will also be important to determine whether the effects
identified here depend strongly on the underlying model architecture or whether similar
principles can be transferred broadly across different AIWP systems.

An important next step will be to replace these boundaries with forecasts from different global
prediction systems and evaluate the resulting sensitivity to boundary uncertainty. The analysis
framework developed for TCs can also be extended to other high-impact weather systems, such
as atmospheric rivers and other mesoscale extremes. Taken together, these results show that
spectrum-aware loss functions offer an effective way to reduce excessive smoothing and better
preserve forecast variability and consistency in deterministic AI weather prediction models.
