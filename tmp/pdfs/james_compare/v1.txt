Abstract 19 
Artificial intelligence weather prediction (AIWP) has demonstrated strong performance in 20 
minimizing pointwise mean squared error (MSE) and predicting tropical cyclone tracks. Despite 21 
these advances, maintaining physical consistency remains a challenge, and existing AIWP 22 
models often substantially underestimate tropical cyclone intensity. These limitations constrain 23 
their applicability to research-oriented atmospheric simulations. This study develops an AIWP 24 
framework that uses a multiscale mesh to represent atmospheric processes across spatial scales 25 
and incorporates a comprehensive set of atmospheric state variables. The model combines a 26 
message-passing graph neural network with linear attention to capture spatial interactions and 27 
long-range dependencies. During training, predictability-dependent filtering and spectral 28 
constraints are introduced to mitigate excessive smoothing and preserve high-frequency features. 29 
The resulting framework improves tropical cyclone intensity forecasts without compromising 30 
track accuracy. Further analysis shows that models trained with spectral constraints better 31 
preserve physical consistency in tropical cyclone inner-core dynamics and thermodynamic 32 
structure. Even without explicit training for longer forecast horizons, the model maintains stable 33 
forecasts and physically realistic structures during extended autoregressive integration. These 34 
results highlight the value of predictability-dependent filtering and spectral constraints for 35 
improving tropical cyclone prediction and suggest their potential applicability to other extreme 36 
weather events and long-term simulation experiments. 37

Plain Language Summary 45 
Artificial intelligence can produce accurate global weather forecasts. However, it often 46 
underestimates tropical cyclone intensity and blurs fine-scale atmospheric features. These 47 
limitations restrict the broader application of AI weather models to studying tropical cyclone 48 
evolution. In this study, we develop an AI weather prediction model that incorporates a 49 
comprehensive set of atmospheric variables. We also design an adjusted loss function to balance 50 
forecast accuracy and atmospheric activity. This approach significantly improves tropical 51 
cyclone intensity forecasts while maintaining track accuracy. It also better preserves physical 52 
consistency, producing stable predictions with realistic cyclone structures. These findings offer a 53 
general framework for improving AI-based tropical cyclone forecasts, with promising 54 
applications to other extreme weather events and extended atmospheric simulations. 55

1 Introduction 68 
Tropical cyclones (TCs) are among the most destructive weather systems, causing substantial 69 
socioeconomic losses and loss of life worldwide. Accurately predicting TC evolution, including 70 
changes in track, intensity, and structure, remains a major forecasting challenge. Reliable 71 
forecasts of these characteristics are therefore essential for anticipating TC-related hazards and 72 
supporting risk assessment and preparedness (Powell & Reinhold, 2007). In parallel with 73 
continued advances in high-resolution physics-based numerical weather prediction (NWP), data-74 
driven deep-learning approaches have emerged as an increasingly important component of 75 
modern weather forecasting. These approaches span a broad range of applications, from 76 
statistical post-processing and downscaling to global autoregressive models capable of producing 77 
medium-range atmospheric forecasts. 78 
 79 
The development of data-driven weather forecasting has proceeded through several distinct 80 
modeling paradigms. Early graph neural network–based systems demonstrated forecast skill 81 
approaching that of state-of-the-art NWP models, despite operating at relatively coarse spatial 82 
resolution (Keisler, 2022). Subsequent studies showed that competitive medium-range forecast 83 
skill could be achieved using markedly different architectures, including adaptive Fourier neural 84 
operators (Pathak et al., 2022), hierarchical three-dimensional Transformer architectures (Bi et 85 
al., 2023), and multiscale graph representations. Importantly, progress has also resulted from 86 
advances in training methodology and data strategy. GraphCast combined multiscale 87 
representations with multi-step rollout training (Lam et al., 2023), whereas Aurora leveraged 88 
heterogeneous data and large-scale pretraining to extend data-driven prediction across multiple 89 
Earth-system applications (Bodnar et al., 2025). Collectively, these developments demonstrate 90 
that high forecast skill can emerge from diverse architectural paradigms and increasingly 91 
highlight the importance of training strategy, objective formulation, and data composition 92 
alongside architectural innovation (Garg et al., 2026). Consequently, further advances in AI 93 
weather prediction may depend not only on how forecasting models are constructed, but also on 94 
which properties of atmospheric variability their training objectives encourage them to retain.  95 
 96 
Although data-driven weather prediction models have achieved substantial improvements in 97 
conventional verification metrics, such as root-mean-square error (RMSE) and anomaly 98

correlation coefficient (ACC), these metrics provide only a partial characterization of forecast 99 
quality (Zhang et al., 2026).  Such aggregate accuracy measures may obscure important forecast 100 
deficiencies, including systematic underestimation of tropical cyclone intensity, biases in storm 101 
structure and cross-scale variability, and increasingly over-smoothed forecast fields at longer 102 
lead times (L. A. Smith & Thorpe, 2026). These deficiencies can substantially limit the practical 103 
value of data-driven models for tropical cyclone forecasting. Despite their highly competitive 104 
track-prediction performance, data-driven models still lag substantially behind operational 105 
guidance in tropical cyclone intensity forecasting  (DeMaria et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024). This 106 
gap in intensity forecasting capability is particularly pronounced in extreme cases that require 107 
stronger generalization and extrapolation beyond commonly observed conditions, such as super 108 
typhoons and tropical cyclone rapid intensification events (Chih et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2025). 109 
 110 
The choice of training objective may contribute to some of these deficiencies, particularly the 111 
tendency toward excessive smoothing and weakened forecast intensity. Most deterministic data-112 
driven weather prediction models are trained predominantly using pointwise mean squared error 113 
(MSE), with minimization of this objective commonly treated as a direct route toward improving 114 
forecast accuracy. However, the conditional mean that minimizes expected MSE does not 115 
necessarily correspond to a physically realizable atmospheric state.  As forecast uncertainty 116 
increases, the conditional distribution broadens, and its mean may increasingly represent an 117 
average over multiple plausible atmospheric evolutions rather than any individual realization 118 
(Selz et al., 2026). For spatially varying weather systems, such averaging can attenuate sharp 119 
gradients and small-scale structures, thereby producing progressively smoother forecast fields. 120 
This limitation can also be understood from a spectral and spatial-error perspective. In a 121 
spherical harmonic representation, MSE can be decomposed into contributions associated with 122 
differences in spectral amplitude and phase (Bouallègue, 2024).  123

 131 
Spatial displacement of a forecast feature is expressed spectrally through phase changes. Even 132 
when the amplitude of a displaced feature is accurately reproduced, pointwise verification 133 
penalizes the forecast both where the observed feature is absent from the forecast and where the 134 
displaced forecast feature appears. This constitutes the familiar double-penalty effect of 135 
pointwise verification. When the location of a feature becomes increasingly uncertain, reducing 136 
its amplitude can lower the expected pointwise squared error by reducing the penalty associated 137 
with misplaced high-amplitude structures. Pointwise MSE optimization can therefore favor 138 
reduced forecast activity as predictability decreases, a behavior commonly described as the 139 
accuracy–activity trade-off (Bouallègue, 2024).  140 
 141 
Consistent with this mechanism, several evaluated AI weather prediction systems exhibit 142 
deficient spectral variability relative to reanalysis, particularly at smaller spatial scales and 143 
longer forecast lead times. This tendency can become especially pronounced in models trained 144 
with multi-step autoregressive rollouts (Philippé et al., 2026), even though autoregressive 145 
curriculum training is commonly employed to improve forecast accuracy and long-term stability 146 
(Bodnar et al., 2025; Lam et al., 2023). For tropical cyclone prediction, improved model design 147 
may partially enhance predictability and mitigate the adverse effects of excessive smoothing; 148 
however, substantial biases remain in the representation of inner-core structure and dynamics 149 
(Weng & Gori, 2026). These include deficiencies in TC intensity, wind–pressure relationships, 150 
radial wind structure, radius of maximum wind, and dynamical balance. Together, these results 151 
suggest that improvements in conventional forecast accuracy do not necessarily imply 152 
corresponding improvements in the structural and dynamical characteristics of tropical cyclones. 153 
 154 
These limitations have motivated training objectives that explicitly account for spectral 155 
properties. NeuralGCM, for example, incorporates lead-time-dependent spectral filtering 156 
together with spectral amplitude and bias constraints within its deterministic training formulation 157 
(Kochkov et al., 2024).  Other approaches include the binned spectral power (BSP) loss, which 158 
constrains the distribution of power across spatial scales (Chakraborty et al., 2026), and the 159 

=== PAGE 7 ===
manuscript submitted to Journal of Advances in Modeling Earth Systems 
 
adjusted mean squared error (AMSE), which separates spectral amplitude errors from 160 
decorrelation errors to reduce the incentive for excessive smoothing (Subich et al., 2025). These 161 
approaches provide important foundations, but preserving spectral power alone does not ensure 162 
accurate feature placement or improved tropical cyclone structure and cross-variable 163 
relationships. Moreover, spectral formulations developed for global domains, particularly those 164 
based on spherical harmonics, require modification when applied to limited-area regional grids 165 
and boundary-forced prediction systems. 166 
 167 
A central unresolved question is therefore whether improved preservation of spectral variability 168 
in a fully data-driven regional forecast model translates into meaningful improvements in 169 
tropical cyclone intensity and structure, and what trade-off, if any, this imposes on pointwise 170 
forecast accuracy. Addressing this question requires more than demonstrating improved power 171 
spectra alone: spectral fidelity must be evaluated together with pointwise forecast error and TC-172 
specific structural diagnostics. 173 
 174 
Our study investigates this accuracy–variability–structure trade-off using a fully data-driven 175 
regional deep-learning framework for the western North Pacific. We develop a regional graph 176 
neural network using ERA5 reanalysis and construct a controlled training framework in which 177 
spectral objectives can be evaluated alongside conventional pointwise optimization (Hersbach et 178 
al., 2020). The model includes multiscale graph representations and a broad set of atmospheric 179 
and surface variables, while the training procedure progressively extends forecast lead time and 180 
introduces predictability-dependent spectral filtering and constraints on spectral variability. We 181 
evaluate whether these objectives improve the representation of tropical cyclones using three 182 
complementary classes of diagnostics: conventional pointwise verification, spectral measures of 183 
atmospheric variability, and TC-specific measures of track, intensity, and storm structure. This 184 
design allows us to determine whether increased spectral activity is accompanied by physically 185 
meaningful improvements in the simulated storms rather than by spectral power increases alone. 186

2 Data and Methodology 194 
2.1 Dataset 195 
The dataset is constructed from ERA5 reanalysis data on a 0.25° latitude–longitude grid 196 
(Hersbach et al., 2020).  To balance computational cost while preserving a sufficiently effective 197 
area for evaluating predictive skill, we use a regional domain rather than a global domain. We 198 
select a temporal subset comprising periods extending three days before and after times when a 199 
tropical cyclone’s best-track position lies within the selected domain. This selection reduces the 200 
number of samples by approximately half while retaining the atmospheric evolution during and 201 
around the tropical cyclone lifetime. The data are divided into separate training, validation, and 202 
test sets, with all evaluations reported in this study conducted on the 2024 test set. Three-203 
dimensional atmospheric variables are represented across 13 pressure levels. Climatological 204 
reference fields used for selected evaluation metrics are obtained from WeatherBench (Rasp et 205 
al., 2024), while tropical cyclone data are obtained from the Joint Typhoon Warning Center 206 
(JTWC) best-track dataset. Further details of the dataset are summarized in Table 1. 207

2.2 Graph and Model 226 
An appropriate spatial representation is essential for modeling atmospheric evolution. Numerical 227 
weather prediction models use configurations such as nested domains in WRF and variable-228 
resolution meshes in MPAS-A to allocate computational resources to regions requiring finer 229 
resolution (Park et al., 2014). In this study, the ERA5 fields are provided on a regular 0.25° 230 
latitude–longitude grid. Although this grid offers a consistent representation of the atmospheric 231 
variables, its physical spacing varies with latitude because meridians converge toward the poles 232 
(Figure 1a and 1b). We therefore distinguish between the grid on which atmospheric inputs and 233 
predictions are defined and the graph representation on which latent features are processed. 234 
 235 
Existing AI weather prediction models employ different spatial representations and 236 
computational architectures. Pangu-Weather and Aurora use three-dimensional architectures 237 
derived from the Swin Transformer (Bi et al., 2023; Bodnar et al., 2025), whereas the spherical 238 
Fourier neural operator approach developed for FourCastNet processes atmospheric fields in the 239 
spectral domain  (Bonev et al., 2023). Here, we adopt an icosahedral graph representation similar 240 
to that used in GraphCast (Lam et al., 2023). Unlike GraphCast, which combines connections 241 
from multiple refinement levels into a multimesh graph, we retain each refinement level as a 242 
separate graph. This design allows latent atmospheric features to be processed at distinct spatial 243 
resolutions and exchanged between levels through explicit interlevel connections. 244 
 245

(Battaglia et al., 2018). In the formulation used here, edge features are updated using the states of 247

The graph hierarchy comprises five icosahedral refinement levels, from levels 3 to 7, containing 255 
38, 156, 618, 2,441, and 9,749 nodes, respectively (Figure 1e and 1i). Together with the latitude–256 
longitude grid, these meshes form a heterogeneous graph with distinct node types and 257 
connectivity patterns. To reduce the truncation of graph neighborhoods near the lateral 258 
boundaries, mesh nodes are retained over an extended domain with a 2.5° buffer around the 259 
atmospheric grid (Figure 1c and 1d). This buffer provides additional mesh nodes for connections 260 
near the domain edges without enlarging the atmospheric prediction domain. 261 
 262 
Connectivity is defined separately for encoding, processing, decoding, and information exchange 263 
between mesh levels. During encoding, grid nodes are connected to level-7 mesh nodes within a 264 
prescribed great-circle distance, transferring atmospheric information from the regular grid to the 265 
mesh (Figure 1j). Within each mesh level, processor edges connect adjacent mesh nodes and 266 
include self-loops; Figure 1k illustrates a level-7 node connected to its six neighbors and itself. 267 
During decoding, each grid node receives information from its three nearest mesh nodes at the 268 
relevant refinement level, projecting the learned mesh features back onto the latitude–longitude 269 
grid (Figure 1l). 270 
 271 
Information exchange between successive mesh levels is implemented through pooling and 272 
upsampling connections in a graph U-Net structure (Gao & Ji, 2022). These connections 273 
associate coarse-level nodes with local neighborhoods on the finer mesh, using seven-node 274 
neighborhoods to transfer features between resolutions. Pooling aggregates fine-level 275 
information onto the coarser mesh, whereas upsampling transfers coarse-level information back 276 
to the finer mesh. Together with message passing within each level, these operations allow the 277 
model to combine information represented at multiple spatial resolutions. 278

Building on this multi-level graph representation, the overall model uses a hierarchical Graph U-298 
Net architecture shown in Figure 2 (Gao & Ji, 2022). The architecture consists of five major 299 
stages: encoding, pooling, processing, upsampling, and decoding. Atmospheric information is 300 
first encoded from the latitude–longitude grid onto the graph, progressively transferred across 301 
different mesh resolutions, processed within the multiscale graph hierarchy, and then propagated 302 
back to the grid through the upsampling and decoding stages. Because atmospheric evolution is 303 
governed by interactions of varying strengths among surrounding atmospheric states, this study 304 
adopts GATv2 (Brody et al., 2021) as the primary graph neural network layer. The module’s 305 
dynamic attention mechanism lets the model adaptively adjust the relative importance of 306 
surrounding atmospheric information based on the evolving atmospheric state. 307 
 308 
In a graph neural network, each layer exchanges information within the first-order neighborhood 309 
defined by the graph connectivity. Stacking multiple GATv2 layers progressively expands the 310 
receptive field (Brody et al., 2021), allowing information to propagate over larger spatial 311 
distances (Sanchez-Lengeling et al., 2021). During the processing stage, a dual-branch design is 312 
introduced to balance computational cost and long-range information exchange. One branch 313 
performs graph-based message passing, while the other employs linear attention to capture 314 
broader spatial interactions (Q. Wu et al., 2023). The outputs of the two branches are then 315 
combined through a gated fusion mechanism. 316 
 317 
Several architectural components are incorporated to improve the effectiveness and stability of 318 
graph-based learning, including edge features, normalization, dropout, residual connections, 319 
feed-forward networks, and positional encodings (Luo et al., 2024, 2025). Layer normalization is 320 
adopted to maintain stable training under relatively small batch sizes (Ba et al., 2016). The feed-321 
forward network is replaced by a gated linear unit to introduce feature-dependent gating and 322 
enhance nonlinear feature transformation. 323

2.3 Training 349 
To achieve an optimal trade-off between physical consistency and accuracy, the model training 350 
procedure is divided into four stages. In the first stage, pre-training is conducted on a coarser-351 
resolution mesh. The second stage fine-tunes the model on a finer-resolution mesh. In the third 352 
stage, the training extends from single-step predictions to autoregressive multi-step rollouts. 353 
Finally, the fourth stage incorporates predictability-based filtering and spectral loss functions 354 
into the optimization objective. The model is trained progressively according to task difficulty 355 
advancing from large-scale background environments to mesoscale details, then to long-term 356 
simulation stability, and ultimately to a physically consistent atmospheric energy distribution. 357 
 358 
The training hyperparameters are summarized in Table 3. The prediction targets are consistently 359 
formulated as increments of the atmospheric state. In addition to area-weight adjustments that 360 
account for latitude-dependent grid distortions, loss weighting incorporates the relative 361 
importance and predictability of different variables. 362 
 363 
For a regional autoregressive model, lateral boundary condition (LBC) treatment is critical for 364 
maintaining simulation stability, as errors at the boundaries can propagate into the interior 365 
domain via advection or wave propagation (Warner et al., 1997). Taking semi-Lagrangian 366 
advection as an example, the lateral boundary buffer must span at least several grid points to 367 
ensure numerical stability (Davies, 2014). In our training configuration, ERA5 lateral boundary 368 
forcing is applied over a nine-grid-point buffer beginning at the second autoregressive step.  369

3 Evaluation 418 
All evaluation results presented in this study are computed over the 2024 test set, following the 419 
tropical-cyclone-centered sampling criterion described in Section 2.1. Forecasts are initialized at 420 
00, 06, 12, and 18 UTC. Three variants of the proposed model are evaluated: the Stage 3 421 
checkpoint trained solely with the weighted MSE objective, and two Stage 4 variants 422 
incorporating the Spectrum-MAE and Spectrum-MSE formulations, respectively. 423 
 424 
For external comparison, Pangu-Weather is selected as the deterministic baseline (Bi et al., 425 
2023). Although more recent deterministic AI weather prediction systems, such as GraphCast 426 
and Aurora (Bodnar et al., 2025; Lam et al., 2023), have demonstrated improved performance in 427 
conventional global forecast metrics, including RMSE, Pangu-Weather remains particularly 428 
suitable for the objectives of this study. It offers lower computational overhead, and previous 429 
studies have extensively examined its tropical cyclone forecasts, providing a solid reference for 430 
interpreting results (DeMaria et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024; Weng & Gori, 2026). Furthermore, 431 
Pangu-Weather does not employ the multi-step autoregressive curriculum training commonly 432 
adopted in later systems and is therefore less affected by the progressive accumulation of 433 
excessive smoothing over extended autoregressive rollouts (Philippé et al., 2026). Its 434 
comparatively moderate spectral dissipation makes it a suitable baseline for evaluating whether 435 
the proposed spectrum-aware objectives can effectively retain mesoscale atmospheric energy and 436

4 Synoptic-Scale Metrics 517 
Among the synoptic-scale atmospheric variables, specific humidity and meridional wind are 518 
selected as representative variables because they directly characterize the moisture distribution 519 
and wind field associated with tropical cyclones. To evaluate the model’s practical skill in 520 
reproducing these aspects of tropical-cyclone structure and environment, the conventional RMSE 521 
and ACC metrics are supplemented with forecast bias and forecast activity. These 522 
complementary metrics are used to distinguish intrinsic forecast skill from improvements that 523 
may arise primarily through calibration. Here, intrinsic skill refers to the model’s ability to 524 
reproduce the observed atmospheric evolution and variability, whereas calibration denotes 525 
improvements that can be achieved through procedures such as smoothing, filtering, or 526 
systematic bias correction (Bonavita & Geer, 2026). 527 
 528 
As shown in Figure 3, after the prescribed lateral boundary conditions begin to influence the 529 
forecasts, our model exhibits RMSE values approximately 10% lower than those of Pangu-530 
Weather, indicating comparable or higher pointwise accuracy under the present evaluation 531 
framework. This advantage initially decreases with forecast lead time and subsequently increases 532 
again, possibly because the influence of the lateral boundary conditions gradually propagates 533 
into the interior of the regional domain. Comparing the different training objectives, both 534 
Spectrum-MAE and Spectrum-MSE generally exhibit higher RMSE than the MSE-trained 535 
model, whereas their effects on ACC are relatively moderate. For specific humidity, both Pangu-536 
Weather and the MSE-trained model show pronounced dry biases at longer lead times, 537 
suggesting that their moisture distributions become progressively less consistent with the 538 
reanalysis field and limiting the fidelity of their moisture forecasts at longer lead times. 539

5 Tropical Cyclone Verification 614 
To quantitatively assess the impact of spectral-loss training on tropical cyclone (TC) forecasts, 615 
we apply the TC tracking algorithm described in Section 3 and evaluate forecasts using a 616 
common-sample framework. Because a regional forecasting system may introduce sampling 617 
biases when TCs enter or leave the model domain, or when storms develop or dissipate during 618 
the forecast period, only cases successfully identified across all four model configurations are 619 
retained. The evaluation is conducted at forecast lead times of 1–3 days, comprising 265, 211, 620 
and 162 forecast samples, respectively, corresponding to 18, 16, and 15 individual TCs. 621 
 622 
The results demonstrate that incorporating spectral loss during training improves TC intensity 623 
prediction while largely preserving track accuracy (Figure 7). TC motion is primarily governed 624 
by large-scale environmental steering flows; consequently, the inclusion of spectral constraints 625 
produces only minor differences in absolute track error. In contrast, TC intensity is more 626 
sensitive to the representation of mesoscale structures. Consistent with the enhanced spectral 627 
energy retention at these scales discussed in the preceding sections, the spectral-loss models 628 
exhibit improved intensity forecasts relative to both the MSE-only baseline and Pangu-Weather, 629 
with substantial reductions in intensity error. 630 
 631 
Beyond the mean error statistics, spectral-loss training also improves the representation of the 632 
distribution of TC intensity changes. In particular, the spectral-loss models better reproduce the 633 
upper tail of the ERA5 intensity-change distribution, whereas pointwise-loss forecasts tend to 634 
underrepresent strongly intensifying cases. Preserving this tail behavior is especially relevant for 635 
forecasts of rapid intensification, for which excessive smoothing of mesoscale structures may 636 
systematically suppress large intensity changes. These results therefore provide TC-scale 637 
evidence that the improved retention of mesoscale spectral energy translates into more accurate 638 
representations of storm intensity and intensification. 639

The 816 intensity bias and loss of forecast consistency associated with excessive smoothing. By 817 comparing different loss-function variants with a pointwise-loss baseline and Pangu-Weather (Bi 818 et al., 2023), we find that spectrum-aware training can retain comparable overall forecast 819 accuracy while producing only a modest increase in RMSE and having a minor impact on TC 820 track prediction. At the same time, it reduces the dry bias in specific humidity, better preserves 821 variability in the wind field, and substantially improves the prediction of TC intensity and 822 intensification process. The subsequent structural diagnostics further demonstrate that the 823 benefits of spectral loss are not limited to improvements in pointwise extreme values. Instead, a 824 broad range of storm-structure parameters are reproduced more consistently with the reanalysis. 825

In the monthly simulations, the spectrum-aware models are able to reproduce the evolution of 826 
multiple TCs under reanalysis-derived lateral boundary forcing. This behavior differs from that 827 
commonly observed in deterministic pointwise-trained models, whose practical value at 828 
extended lead times can be substantially reduced as progressive smoothing suppresses mesoscale 829 
variability.  830 
 831 
The comparison between the two spectral-loss formulations does not indicate an unambiguous 832 
overall winner, particularly because their normalization schemes are not completely identical. 833 
Nevertheless, the results reveal distinct characteristics. The spectrum-MAE formulation is better 834 
suited to preserving medium-range forecast accuracy and high-wavenumber sharpness, whereas 835 
the spectrum-MSE formulation retains the overall spectral-energy distribution more effectively 836 
during long autoregressive integrations, at the cost of larger degradation in conventional 837 
accuracy metrics. This contrast further illustrates the trade-off between pointwise forecast 838 
accuracy and the preservation of dynamically relevant variability and consistency (Bouallègue, 839 
2024). A possible direction for future work is therefore to combine or transition between these 840 
objectives across different forecast time scales. Broadly, the results suggest that training 841 
objectives and data impact forecast behavior just as strongly as model architecture (Garg et al., 842 
2026). 843 
 844 
Several limitations should nevertheless be considered when interpreting these results. The 845 
forecasts examined here remain constrained by reanalysis-derived lateral boundary forcing. 846 
Consequently, the experimental framework is more comparable to a regional WRF-style 847 
simulation than to a fully operational forecasting system, limiting direct comparisons of absolute 848 
forecast accuracy with global operational models. In addition, the primary references for TC 849 
intensity and structural diagnostics are derived from reanalysis. Because the effective resolution 850 
of the reanalysis is insufficient to fully resolve the most intense and compact TC inner-core 851 
structures, these reference fields also introduce uncertainty into the evaluation. Despite these 852 
limitations, the results provide useful guidance for the further development of regional AIWP 853 
systems and spectrum-aware training strategies. 854

relative to the original pointwise-accuracy objective, several aspects of the training framework 857 
remain dependent on manually specified choices. Future work should therefore aim to reduce 858 
this dependence, including the selection of the filtering cutoff wavenumber, the relative 859

assigned to different forecast variables. It will also be important to determine whether the effects 861 
identified here depend strongly on the underlying model architecture or whether similar 862 
principles can be transferred broadly across different AIWP systems. 863 
 864

spectrum-aware loss functions offer an effective way to reduce excessive smoothing and better 869
